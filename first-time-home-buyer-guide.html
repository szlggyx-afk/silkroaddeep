First-Time Home Buyer Guide: 10 Steps to Buying Your First Home

By CalcWise Team · August 13, 2026 · 12 min read

Buying your first home is one of the most exciting—and most intimidating—financial decisions you'll ever make. It's a major milestone that builds wealth, provides stability, and gives you a place to call your own. But the process can feel overwhelming, with unfamiliar terms, complex paperwork, and significant financial stakes.

This guide walks you through every step of the home buying process, from preparing your finances to getting the keys to your new front door. Whether you're just starting to think about buying or you're ready to start house hunting, you'll find practical, actionable advice here.

Step 1: Assess Your Financial Readiness

Before you start browsing listings, take an honest look at your finances. Ask yourself:

  • Do I have a steady income? — Lenders want to see at least 2 years of consistent employment history.
  • Is my credit score in good shape? — A score of 620+ is typically needed for a conventional loan; 740+ gets you the best rates. Check our guide on improving your credit score if yours needs work.
  • Do I have savings for a down payment and closing costs? — Down payments can be as low as 3% (conventional) or 3.5% (FHA), but you'll also need 2%–5% of the home price for closing costs.
  • Can I afford the monthly payment? — As a rule of thumb, your total housing payment (PITI) should be no more than 28% of your gross monthly income.
  • Do I have an emergency fund? — Aim for 3–6 months of expenses saved before buying, so unexpected home repairs don't derail your finances.

Step 2: Determine How Much House You Can Afford

One of the biggest mistakes first-time buyers make is falling in love with a home they can't truly afford. Just because a lender pre-approves you for a certain amount doesn't mean you should borrow that much.

Consider the full cost of homeownership, not just the mortgage payment:

  • Principal and interest — The base mortgage payment
  • Property taxes — Average 1.1% of home value annually, but varies by location
  • Homeowners insurance — Average $1,500/year
  • PMI — Required if your down payment is less than 20%
  • HOA fees — Common in condos and planned communities
  • Maintenance and repairs — Budget 1%–2% of the home price per year
  • Utilities — Often higher than renting, especially for larger homes

Lenders use two ratios to qualify you:

  • Front-end ratio — Housing costs ÷ gross monthly income (should be ≤ 28%)
  • Back-end ratio — Housing costs + all other debt payments ÷ gross monthly income (should be ≤ 36%, though some lenders allow up to 43%)

Step 3: Save for a Down Payment and Closing Costs

The down payment is the portion of the home price you pay upfront. While 20% was once the standard, many first-time buyers put down much less:

  • Conventional loans — As little as 3% down (PMI required below 20%)
  • FHA loans — 3.5% down with a credit score of 580+
  • VA loans — 0% down for eligible veterans and service members
  • USDA loans — 0% down for eligible rural and suburban properties

Closing costs typically run 2%–5% of the home price and include appraisal fees, title insurance, origination fees, and prepaid taxes and insurance. You may be able to negotiate with the seller to cover some closing costs, or use a down payment assistance program.

First-time buyer programs: Many states, cities, and nonprofits offer down payment assistance, tax credits, and favorable loan terms for first-time buyers. Research programs in your area—some provide grants of up to $15,000 or more that don't need to be repaid.

Step 4: Get Pre-Approved for a Mortgage

A mortgage pre-approval is a lender's written estimate of how much they're willing to lend you, based on a review of your income, assets, and credit. It shows sellers that you're a serious, qualified buyer—and in competitive markets, many sellers won't consider offers without one.

Pre-qualification vs. pre-approval: A pre-qualification is an informal estimate based on self-reported information. A pre-approval involves a hard credit check and verification of your financial documents, making it much more credible.

To get pre-approved, you'll need:

  • Recent pay stubs (last 30 days)
  • W-2 forms (last 2 years)
  • Tax returns (last 2 years)
  • Bank statements (last 2–3 months)
  • Proof of any additional income (alimony, bonuses, etc.)
  • ID and Social Security number

Get pre-approved with at least 2–3 lenders to compare rates and terms. Each pre-approval within a 14–45 day window counts as a single credit inquiry, so shopping around won't significantly hurt your score.

Step 5: Find the Right Real Estate Agent

A good buyer's agent is invaluable, especially for first-time buyers. They'll guide you through the process, negotiate on your behalf, and help you avoid costly mistakes. Best of all, the seller typically pays the buyer's agent commission (usually 2.5%–3% of the sale price), so their services are effectively free to you.

Look for an agent who:

  • Specializes in the neighborhoods where you want to buy
  • Has experience working with first-time buyers
  • Is responsive and communicates in your preferred style
  • Has positive reviews from past clients
  • Is a full-time agent (not part-time)

Interview at least 2–3 agents before choosing one. Ask about their experience, how they handle multiple-offer situations, and what sets them apart.

Step 6: Start House Hunting

Now comes the fun part—shopping for homes. Before you start touring, make a list of your must-haves and nice-to-haves:

  • Must-haves — Non-negotiable features (e.g., 3 bedrooms, specific school district, garage)
  • Nice-to-haves — Features you'd love but can compromise on (e.g., updated kitchen, backyard, hardwood floors)
  • Deal-breakers — Things you absolutely won't accept (e.g., busy street, flood zone, too much repair needed)

Tips for house hunting:

  • Focus on the home's layout and location, not cosmetic details like paint colors or outdated fixtures—those are easy to change
  • Pay attention to structural issues: foundation cracks, water stains, roof condition, electrical and plumbing
  • Visit at different times of day to check noise levels, traffic, and natural light
  • Research the neighborhood: crime rates, schools, amenities, property values, and future development plans
  • Don't wait for the "perfect" home—most buyers compromise on something. The goal is to find a home that meets your needs and fits your budget.

Step 7: Make an Offer

When you find a home you love, your agent will help you craft a competitive offer. Your offer should include:

  • Purchase price — Based on comparable sales in the area and the home's condition
  • Earnest money deposit — Typically 1%–3% of the purchase price, held in escrow as a sign of good faith
  • Contingencies — Conditions that must be met for the sale to proceed (financing, inspection, appraisal)
  • Closing date — Usually 30–45 days from offer acceptance
  • Inclusions and exclusions — What appliances and fixtures stay with the home

In a competitive market, you may need to offer above asking price, waive some contingencies (carefully), or include a personal letter to the seller. Your agent will advise you on the best strategy based on local market conditions.

Step 8: Schedule a Home Inspection

Once your offer is accepted, schedule a professional home inspection within the contingency period (usually 7–14 days). A licensed inspector will evaluate the home's major systems and structure, including:

  • Roof and exterior
  • Foundation and structural components
  • Electrical system
  • Plumbing system
  • HVAC (heating, ventilation, air conditioning)
  • Interior condition (walls, floors, ceilings)
  • Appliances included in the sale

The inspection typically costs $300–$500 and is well worth it. If the inspection reveals significant issues, you can:

  • Ask the seller to make repairs before closing
  • Negotiate a credit or price reduction to cover the cost of repairs
  • Walk away from the deal (within the inspection contingency period) without losing your earnest money

Step 9: Secure Your Mortgage and Appraisal

After the inspection, it's time to finalize your mortgage. Your lender will order an appraisal to confirm the home's value is at least the purchase price—this protects both you and the lender. If the appraisal comes in low, you may need to renegotiate the price, make a larger down payment, or walk away.

During underwriting, avoid:

  • Making large purchases (new car, furniture, appliances)
  • Opening new credit accounts or closing existing ones
  • Changing jobs
  • Depositing large cash amounts without a paper trail

Any of these could affect your debt-to-income ratio or credit score and jeopardize your loan approval. Wait until after closing to make major financial changes.

Step 10: Close on Your New Home

Closing day is when the property officially becomes yours. You'll attend a closing meeting (often at a title company or attorney's office) where you'll:

  • Review and sign all loan documents (the promissory note, deed of trust, and disclosures)
  • Pay your closing costs and down payment (via wire transfer or certified check)
  • Verify that all repairs agreed upon were completed
  • Do a final walk-through of the home (usually 24 hours before closing)

Once all documents are signed and funds are transferred, you'll receive the keys to your new home. Congratulations—you're a homeowner!

First-Time Home Buyer Mistakes to Avoid

  • Buying more house than you can afford — Just because you're approved for $400K doesn't mean you should spend that much. Leave room in your budget for savings and unexpected expenses.
  • Skipping the inspection — Even new homes can have issues. An inspection can save you from costly surprises.
  • Not shopping for a mortgage — A 0.5% rate difference on a $300K loan costs over $30,000 over 30 years.
  • Draining your savings — Keep an emergency fund after closing. Home repairs and unexpected costs are inevitable.
  • Making big purchases before closing — New furniture or a car can change your debt-to-income ratio and kill the deal.
  • Ignoring the neighborhood — You can change the house, but you can't change the location. Research schools, commute times, and amenities.

Bottom Line

Buying your first home is a process, not an event. By preparing your finances, getting pre-approved, working with the right agent, and staying within your budget, you can navigate the process with confidence. Remember that no home is perfect, and the goal is to find a place that meets your needs and sets you up for long-term financial success.
